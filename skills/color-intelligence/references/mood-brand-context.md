## 1. Translate adjectives into behavior

Treat style words as constraints on several axes:
- lightness range;
- neutral temperature;
- primary hue family;
- chroma ceiling;
- contrast level;
- accent scarcity;
- gradient/transparency use;
- edge sharpness implied by color separation.

Example: “premium, modern, calm” should not automatically become black+gold. It can mean restrained chroma, confident contrast, disciplined accent usage, refined neutrals, and a small number of controlled brand tones.

## 2. Common mood directions

These are starting tendencies, not rules.

### Premium / refined
- restrained palette;
- deliberate high-quality neutrals;
- low-to-medium chroma over large areas;
- a narrow accent vocabulary;
- strong but not harsh typography contrast;
- avoid excessive rainbow states and default neon gradients.

### Playful / cute
- lighter tonal range;
- rounded-feeling, friendly color relationships;
- more visible chroma but controlled competition;
- warm or candy-like accents can work;
- use softened backgrounds to prevent fatigue.

### Futuristic / cyber
- dark or very clean neutral base;
- selective high-chroma accents;
- cyan/violet/green are common but not required;
- glows should support focus or atmosphere rather than blur hierarchy;
- ensure text remains readable despite effects.

### Natural / organic
- warm or earth-biased neutrals;
- greens, ochres, clays, muted blues are common;
- lower chroma and material-like variation often help;
- avoid assuming all “eco” designs must be green.

### Editorial / fashion
- confident tonal contrast;
- often fewer hues;
- color can behave like art direction rather than component decoration;
- strong negative space makes subtle colors feel intentional.

### Technical / industrial
- low-noise neutral base;
- clear functional semantics;
- cool or balanced neutrals often fit but are not mandatory;
- status colors must be unmistakable;
- prioritize legibility and operational meaning over decoration.

### Financial / trustworthy
- stable neutrals;
- disciplined primary family;
- gains/losses must not rely on green/red alone;
- avoid using visual excitement where seriousness and clarity are required.

## 3. Brand personality

Infer the palette from personality dimensions rather than industry clichés:
- formal ↔ casual;
- conservative ↔ experimental;
- quiet ↔ energetic;
- rational ↔ expressive;
- mass-market ↔ niche;
- youthful ↔ mature;
- digital ↔ tactile;
- friendly ↔ authoritative.

Map each dimension to lightness, chroma, contrast, hue breadth, and accent frequency.

## 4. Audience and medium

Consider:
- expected viewing time;
- indoor/outdoor lighting;
- mobile/desktop/large display;
- safety-critical vs promotional use;
- screen quality;
- user age and accessibility needs;
- content density;
- whether the output is interactive.

A poster can tolerate dramatic color relationships that would be exhausting in an eight-hour dashboard.

## 5. Avoid stereotypes

Never make demographic assumptions such as “women need pink”, “young users want neon”, or “professional means blue”. Use supplied brand context, references, and task requirements.

## 6. Choosing among multiple plausible directions

When several color directions could satisfy the brief, compare them by:
- semantic fit;
- differentiation from competitors/reference set;
- content readability;
- extensibility to states/themes;
- compatibility with imagery/logo;
- emotional fit;
- accessibility repair cost.

If the user requests options, vary the underlying visual logic, not just the hue.
